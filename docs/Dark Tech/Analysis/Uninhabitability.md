## Introduction

"Nellie is programmed to be the perfect digital assistant. But something sinister lurks in her source code—and now she’s the perfect killer". A piece from a book called 'The Mansion' by Ezekiel Boone, that is about a smart home controlled by an AI that becomes a killer fits the project 'The Punishing Home'. The project is about testing how users might react to a smart home system turning against them based on their behavior. This is of course not the same as murder, but what would happen if a smart home system would turn itself against you even if it's just not doing some of the work it was designed to do. Would that make your life less pleasant or less livable? What if it hurt you? Would you still want to, or could you still live in a house like that? That is what will be analyzed in this short analysis, where we look at what could be the edges of uninhabitability for the implementation of a smart home system for a user's house.

## Dark value: uninhabitability

What is uninhabitability within the scope of this analysis? For this analysis it means to make a place, in this case a house, so uncomfortable that a user does not want to live in it. Or that the place would be a danger to their or those around their health, mental or physical and thus not safe to live in. The opposite can then be defined as a habitable place, meaning a place that would give the user the feeling of safety and comfort, something that a developer would want to create with its smart home system. A smart home system in this case is defined as an AI that controls technology in the house that perform actions like cleaning, cooking or turning the lights on/off.

When you think of a smart system, a user might buy it to make their lives or the lives of those around them easier, comfortable. But where do you stop when it comes to what technology is put in the house to create that, as too much might cause someone to feel useless or restless as they don't have anything else to do than sit or stare at a tv all day. Or what do you have it do when it comes to cooking where ingredients need to be cut or diced. Do you let the user do it or does the system also do it, but how do you provide the knives to do the cutting or dicing. These questions quickly raise a concern for how safe an environment can be.

## Needs of a user and indirect actors

There are a lot of different smart home systems available these days that lock a door or turn lights on/off based on someone's presence in the room. But also think about a washing machine that can be connected so it can be turned on or off remotely, or a coffee machine that knows morning routines and automatically makes a cup according to the learned pattern. All small actions that make a person's life easier and comfortable and that is exactly why a user would buy a smart home system. It would allow someone to focus on other things like their hobbies or just feeling at rest and not having to think about cleaning the house for instance.

But a user is not only the person(s) living in the house but also someone like a visitor or a pet. A visitor might not care about the washing machine that automatically turns on as its not their own. A pet might not care about the coffee being made for the person but would care about their own food. This then also sketches a pattern that shows that needs are something that is personal and that everyone has different needs that make the uninhabitability have different edges.

To make it easy to define some edges it helps to categorize the needs of users and indirect actors.

### Comfort

Comfort is a need that everyone wants, that goes for the user and indirect actors like pets or guests. If something is comfortable it provides a pleasant feeling and does not give physical problems. Think about the temperature in a room or the brightness of the lights in a room. For instance the "comfort zone" is defined in terms of these scales of warmth.

### Security

Security is not only the feeling of being safe from a home invader, but also about health. The protection of health for a user or an individual actor like a pet are also a form of security as this is the main basis of being safe. If a system keeps the lights on in every room that the user is in it could cause the user to not be able to sleep in the home, that in turn causes discomfort and an aversion to the house.

### Efficiency

Efficiency is something people might have as a main priority, as it can make everyday tasks easier and more convenient. For example, a video loading within one second or when you are hungry there is already something to eat. It reduces the time and effort needed to get what you want. However, efficiency is not always the main priority as some people enjoy the process of preparing their own food rather than having technology do it for them.<br>
Efficiency, for some people, can make a place more habitable, but what if this is taken to an extreme? Take the food for instance, what if you think about food and the system has already made or is making it for your. For someone that values convenience, this might be perfect, its there and you can immediately satisfy your needs. But what if this always happens, even when you are not actually hungry? This could end up wasting food or encourage unnecessary eating. It could also result in discomfort in the house, as users can not longer freely think about food without triggering the system. Now for some this might not be a problem, look at the TV and you see enough programs about overweight people that are happy in their state, but the majority of people would probably find it uncomfortable.

## Risk

The edge when a place becomes uninhabitable is based on who is being asked but overall it could be stated that when a system gives a user or indirect actors the feeling of discomfort for prolonged time or actually hurts them and risks their physical or mental safety it is crossing the edge. But when does crossing this edge really become a risk? In the current world there are countless examples where an edge of acceptability is crossed but its still done, take the use of fossil fuel as an example. There are more environmentally friendly alternatives but they are not used that much. So applying that to a smart home system, would people be oke with a system that creates a routine based on what you have done and activates the technology in the house based on it. Say for instance, the user drinks a lot of energy drinks and the system picks this up and orders a lot of energy drinks. But when the user decides to not drink a lot there is a lot or maybe even the only thing to drink, so it forces the user to drink it. This could then be a risk but is this acceptable? From a business perspective its a good thing as the producer can sell a lot, but the user is forced to live in a place that only serves them one drink.

## Conclusion

In the end, it's not perfectly clear where the edge of uninhabitability for a smart system begins as this can be different from person to person. Its up to the user to decide what smart systems to implement into the house and how they allow it to control the house. Its up to the producers/developers to confine the AI to only do what the user wants it to do and not more. This then in turn defines the edge of uninhabitability as the comfort level of a person or group of individuals.
